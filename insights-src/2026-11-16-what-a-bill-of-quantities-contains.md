---
title: What a builder's bill of quantities actually contains
summary: The quantities are the part everyone looks at. The qualifications are the part that decides whether the bill can be relied on when the tender is lodged.
---
A bill of quantities is usually described as a list of measured items: so many cubic metres of concrete, so many square metres of plasterboard, so many tonnes of reinforcement. That description is accurate, and it misses most of what makes a bill worth commissioning.

The quantities are only as useful as the basis on which they were measured. A builder pricing a tender needs to know not only how much, but *from what*, *on what assumptions*, and *with what left out*. That information sits in the parts of the bill that are rarely read first, and it is where a bill earns its keep.

## The four parts of a builder's bill

### 1. The document register

Every bill should begin by stating exactly which documents were measured: each drawing by number and revision, each schedule, specification and consultant report. This is not an administrative formality. It fixes the scope of the bill to a defined set of documents, so that when revision C of a drawing arrives two days before tender close, everyone can see immediately that the bill was measured from revision B and which items are affected.

A bill without a register cannot be checked, and cannot be updated with any confidence.

### 2. The measured quantities, arranged by trade

The quantities themselves should be set out in the builder's own trade breakdown, so that each trade section can be issued directly to the subcontractors pricing it. Each item carries a description sufficient to price from, a unit and a quantity, and a reference back to where it was measured.

Two principles matter here. First, items are *measured*, not *allowed for*: where something is shown, it is taken off and quantified. Second, where something cannot yet be measured, it is not quietly folded into a neighbouring rate. It is carried as a separate item and identified as such.

### 3. The qualifications

This is the part that decides whether a bill can be relied on. Documentation is never complete at tender. Joinery is shown diagrammatically, temporary works are not detailed, a hazardous materials report refers to "assumed" quantities, and the services drawings are schematic.

A well-prepared bill does not pretend otherwise. Where the documents are silent, it states the assumption that was adopted. Where an element cannot be measured, it carries a provisional item and says so. Where a trade is not measured in detail, such as the mechanical or electrical services, it is included as a coverall item for pricing by the relevant subcontractor, and any quantities given against it are for information only.

The result is a schedule of every judgement made in preparing the bill, in one place, before the tender is lodged rather than after the contract is signed.

### 4. The risk register and the value schedule

The final part looks forward. The risk register identifies the items in which the documentation, the site or the program expose the tender figure: the retaining wall with no geotechnical report, the façade system specified by performance only, the staging that assumes access the site may not provide. Each is named so that it can be priced deliberately rather than absorbed into a general contingency.

The value schedule identifies where an alternative material, system or method would satisfy the design intent at lower cost. For a builder, that is not only a saving. It is something to offer the client that the competing submissions may not have identified.

## Why the order matters

A builder reading a bill for the first time will go straight to the quantities, because that is where the price is. The better habit is to read the register and the qualifications first. They tell you what the quantities are worth.

> A bill of quantities is of value only if the builder can confidently rely on it on tender day. That reliance is earned one qualification at a time.

Two bills with identical concrete quantities can lead to very different tenders. The one that states its assumptions lets the estimator price the risk. The one that does not leaves the estimator to discover it after award.

## What to ask for

When you commission a bill, whether priced or unpriced, ask whether it will include:

- a register of every document measured, with revisions;
- quantities in your own trade breakdown, ready for issue to subcontractors;
- a schedule of assumptions, clarifications, provisional items and coverall sums;
- a risk register and a value schedule;
- notification of evident discrepancies between the drawings and the reports.

If the answer to any of these is no, the bill may still be accurate. It will simply be harder to rely on.
