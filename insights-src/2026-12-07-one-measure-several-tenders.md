---
title: One measure, several tenders: how shared bills of quantities work
summary: When several builders tender the same project, each usually measures it separately. A shared bill lets them price from one measure, at a fraction of the cost, without any builder seeing another's price.
---
On a typical competitive tender, three or four builders each receive the same drawings and each spend days measuring them. The quantities they arrive at should be identical. In practice they differ, sometimes materially, because each estimator interpreted the same documents slightly differently, missed something different, or ran out of time in a different trade.

The client then compares tenders that are priced on different scopes, and the builders have collectively paid several times over for the same measurement.

A shared bill of quantities addresses both problems.

## How it works

The project is measured once. The same bill, with the same document register, qualifications, risk register and value schedule, is issued to each builder who engages us for that tender. Each builder then applies its own rates, preliminaries and margin.

The fee is shared. A bill commissioned by one builder carries the full fee; where a second or third builder engages us for the same bill, the fee per builder falls accordingly. The basis is set out in the fee proposal before any work starts, so each builder knows what it will pay in each case.

## What each builder receives

Each builder receives an identical bill and identical schedules, packaged in its own trade breakdown where that has been requested. Queries raised during the tender period are answered by reference to the bill, and where a clarification changes the measurement, the change is issued to every builder holding the bill, so that no builder is pricing from a different scope.

## What each builder does not receive

No builder sees another's identity through us, its pricing, its subcontractor quotations or its correspondence. The bill is a measurement of the documents, and it is the same for everyone. Everything that distinguishes one tender from another, including rates, buying, program, preliminaries, margin and the alternatives a builder chooses to offer, remains entirely the builder's own.

That is the important distinction. A shared bill removes the duplicated effort, not the competition.

## Why the industry has argued for it

The use of bills of quantities on Australian projects declined as design-and-construct procurement became common, and the work of measurement moved from a single quantity surveyor acting for the client to each tendering builder separately. The Australian Institute of Quantity Surveyors has argued publicly for the bill's return, on the grounds that a single measure reduces the duplicated effort across tenderers and gives clients a like-for-like comparison.

A shared builder's bill is a practical version of the same idea, initiated by the builders rather than the client.

## When it makes sense

A shared bill works best where:

- the documentation is reasonably complete at tender, so that a single measure will hold for every builder;
- the tender period is short, and measurement time is the constraint;
- the builders would otherwise each pay for a bill, or tie up an estimator for a week, to arrive at the same quantities.

It makes less sense on design-and-construct tenders where each builder is expected to develop the design differently, because the scope itself will differ between tenders.

## For subcontractors

The same principle applies one level down. A subcontractor pricing a trade package for several head contractors on the same project can do so from a single measured package, rather than re-measuring the trade for each.

## The practical effect

For the builder, the cost of a complete measure falls substantially, and the estimator's time goes into pricing, buying and strategy rather than take-off. For the client, the tenders it receives are priced on the same scope and are easier to compare. For the project, fewer scope gaps survive into the contract.

If you are tendering a project and suspect the other tenderers are measuring the same drawings, it is worth asking whether a shared bill is available.
