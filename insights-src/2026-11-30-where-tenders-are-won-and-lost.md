---
title: Where tenders are won and lost: an introduction to conversion strategy
summary: A tender is not an estimate with a margin added. It is a document prepared to be accepted, and the decisions that make it acceptable are made long before the summary sheet.
---
Ask an estimator why a tender was lost and the answer is usually "price". Sometimes that is true. More often, the price was the last thing the client saw, not the reason for the decision.

Tenders are compared on price, but they are *chosen* on confidence: confidence that the scope is complete, that the qualifications are reasonable, that the figure will hold, and that the builder understood the job. A tender that is slightly higher but plainly complete will often be preferred to a lower one that raises questions. And a tender that wins on price but has not been prepared properly converts into a contract that loses money.

We call the discipline of preparing a tender with the award, and the margin, in view *conversion strategy*. It is not a separate service. It is the purpose behind every bill and estimate we prepare.

## Two conversions, not one

There are two conversions in every tender, and both have to succeed.

**The bid converts into a contract.** The client and their advisers have to be able to compare the submission with others, understand what is and is not included, and accept it without a long round of clarifications.

**The contract converts into the margin that was tendered.** After award, the figure has to survive the reality of the documents, the site and the program. Every scope gap discovered after signing comes out of the margin.

A tender that achieves the first and fails the second is not a win. It is a loss deferred.

## Where conversion is decided

The decisions that determine both conversions are made early, mostly while the documents are being measured.

### A complete scope

The single most common cause of margin erosion is scope that was not priced because it was not seen. A complete measurement of every trade, rather than allowances for the difficult ones, removes the largest source of post-award surprises. It also gives the client something they cannot get from a lump sum without a breakdown: evidence that the work has been understood.

### Risks named and priced where they can be seen

As we wrote last week, a single contingency percentage hides every risk behind one number. Naming them lets each be priced, excluded or qualified on its merits. Clients read a clear set of qualifications as competence, not as caution.

### Value the competition has not found

Most tenders compete on the same scope at different prices. A tender that also offers an alternative, such as a structural system, a façade product or a construction sequence that meets the design intent at lower cost, competes on something else. It gives the client a reason to talk to you rather than the next name on the list.

### A submission that can be defended line by line

When a tender is examined, whether by a quantity surveyor acting for the client or by the client's own team, every figure should be capable of substantiation. A builder who can show the quantity, the rate and the assumption behind any line in the tender is in a far stronger position in the post-tender interview than one who can only say the price is "about right".

## What it is not

Conversion strategy is not about winning at any price. A tender that buys the job by under-pricing it has converted the first time and will fail the second. Nor is it about adding clever wording to an otherwise ordinary submission. It is the cumulative effect of a complete measure, a clear set of qualifications and a price that can be explained.

## Why it applies whether or not we price the bill

Many of our clients price the bill themselves. The principle still holds. A bill prepared with conversion in view hands the estimator a complete scope, a register of the risks and a schedule of the value opportunities. The client pricing its own bill does so from a scope that has already been prepared to be won.
