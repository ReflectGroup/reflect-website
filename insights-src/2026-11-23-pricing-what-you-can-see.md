---
title: Pricing what you can see: the risk register and the contingency line
summary: A single contingency percentage hides every risk in the tender behind one number. A risk register names them, so that each can be priced, excluded or qualified on its merits.
---
Most tenders carry a contingency. It usually appears as a percentage near the bottom of the summary sheet, chosen by experience, adjusted by instinct, and occasionally trimmed at the last minute when the total looks too high.

There is nothing wrong with a contingency. The difficulty is what it conceals. A single percentage answers the question "how much risk is in this job?" without ever asking "which risks, and where?". When the tender is won, nobody can say which part of the margin was set aside for the retaining wall and which for the late façade design. When it is lost, nobody can say whether the contingency was the reason.

## What a risk register does differently

A risk register replaces one number with a list. Each entry names a specific exposure in the documentation, the site or the program, and says why it is an exposure. Typical entries look like this:

| Item | Why it is a risk | How it might be dealt with |
|---|---|---|
| Bulk excavation | No geotechnical report; rock level unknown | Provisional quantity for rock; qualify the tender |
| Façade | Performance specification only, no system nominated | Price a nominated system; state it |
| Joinery | Diagrammatic drawings; no finishes schedule | Provisional sum per unit type |
| Staging | Program assumes access via the neighbouring lot | Confirm access before tender, or price the alternative |

Nothing on that list is unusual. What changes is that each item is now visible, and each can be dealt with on its own merits rather than averaged into a percentage.

## Three ways to deal with a named risk

Once a risk has a name, the estimator has three choices, and each is better than a guess.

**Price it.** Where the risk can be quantified, such as a provisional quantity of rock excavation or an allowance for a nominated façade system, it becomes a measured or provisional item with its own rate. The client can see it, and so can the builder's own team after award.

**Exclude it.** Where the risk belongs with the client, such as contamination beyond the extent shown in the report, it is excluded from the tender and stated as an exclusion. That protects the margin without inflating the price.

**Qualify it.** Where the risk depends on information the builder does not yet have, the tender is qualified: the price assumes a stated condition, and if the condition proves otherwise the price is adjusted. A qualified tender is not a weaker tender. It is a more honest one, and clients and their advisers can tell the difference.

## Why this produces a sharper price

It is tempting to think that naming every risk makes a tender more expensive. In practice it tends to do the opposite.

A general contingency has to be large enough to cover everything the estimator is worried about, including the risks that are unlikely to occur. A register lets each risk be sized individually. The improbable ones are excluded or qualified rather than priced in full, and the ones that are priced are priced at a level that can be defended. The total is often lower, and it is always easier to explain.

There is also a commercial benefit after award. A register prepared at tender becomes the first reference when a variation or a claim arises. If the rock was priced as a provisional quantity, the adjustment is a matter of measurement rather than negotiation.

## Where the register comes from

The register should be prepared alongside the measurement, not after it. The person measuring the documents is the person best placed to see where they are silent, inconsistent or incomplete. A risk noted while the drawings are open is far more specific than one recalled from memory the night before tender close.

That is why every bill of quantities we issue carries a risk register alongside the schedule of assumptions, whether or not we are engaged to price it. The builder pricing the bill receives not only the quantities but a list of the places where the tender figure is exposed.

## A practical test

Before your next tender is lodged, look at the contingency line and ask one question: *could you list what it is for?* If the answer is a handful of specific items, those items belong in the bill, priced, excluded or qualified. If the answer is "general risk", the tender is carrying a number nobody can defend.
